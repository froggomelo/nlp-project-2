\section{Abstract}
{
\bf
\looseness=-1
\emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} (1884--1894) printed an isiXhosa and a smaller English section in every issue,
so comparing them asks what one editor gave two readerships. We examine two choices usually treated as
neutral: how language identity is removed from multilingual embeddings, and whether 1880s isiXhosa
spelling is modernised first. We estimate LaBSE's language subspace only from translated pairs and
measure the content gap as a section classifier's balanced accuracy above a translated-pair floor; on
synthetic data this recovers the planted gap, while mean-centring reports zero. A lexicon-gated
normaliser restores unwritten aspiration and joins separated prefixes, rewriting the names of peoples,
places and God (\emph{Tixo}$\to$\emph{Thixo}). \TBD{H1: the content gap on raw text, with CI.}
\TBD{H2: whether modernisation changes the gap.} We recommend that analyses of historical
African-language text report a normalisation-sensitivity check.
}

\small
\textbf{Keywords:}
historical text normalisation; multilingual sentence embeddings; isiXhosa

\section{Introduction} \label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
\emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded by John Tengo Jabavu in 1884, was the first African-owned and run
newspaper in South Africa. It was written mainly in isiXhosa, but also printed an English translation of
the editorial and, at times, other English articles, letters and advertisements \citep{schoots}. Each
issue therefore addressed two readerships under one editor. Comparing them with NLP tools invites
``cleaning'' the isiXhosa half first by modernising its spelling: the archive writes \emph{Tixo}, \emph{apa} and \emph{umteto} where modern
isiXhosa writes \emph{Thixo}, \emph{apha} and \emph{umthetho}.

\textbf{Research question.} \emph{When Imvo's isiXhosa and English sections from the same issue are
placed in one multilingual embedding space, how distinguishable is their content, and how does
modernising the isiXhosa spelling change that answer?} \textbf{H1:} with language identity removed, a
classifier predicts a chunk's section above a translated-pair floor, i.e.\ the readerships received
different content. \textbf{H2:} modernisation lowers the floor but leaves the gap unchanged; a changed
gap would mean a preprocessing choice alters the conclusion.

\subsubsection{Background}
\looseness=-1
The isiXhosa Intellectual Traditions archive was OCR-corrected by first-language isiXhosa speakers and
deliberately not spell-checked, because the orthography of the period differs from present-day
isiXhosa \citep{schoots}. Historical normalisation is evaluated by accuracy or downstream scores
\citep{bollmann}, not by its effect on conclusions, which makes it a forking path \citep{gelman}. Multilingual encoders such as LaBSE \citep{labse}
still encode language identity. It can be removed by subtracting each language's mean
\citep{libovicky} or by projecting out a low-rank subspace \citep{lir,inlp}. For a linear probe,
mean-centring also removes average content differences, the very thing we measure, so we estimate the
subspace from translated pairs only.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} We use language-split text for 511 of the 517 Imvo issues in our copy (the archive lists
518) with $\geq$1{,}000 words per language: 3.9M isiXhosa and 1.5M English words, close to the archive's
counts \citep{schoots}. We repair OCR artefacts but never spelling, cut pages into 60-word chunks, and
drop boilerplate ($\geq$30\% of 5-word sequences recurring in $\geq$5 issues; 29.9\% of isiXhosa and
7.9\% of English chunks) and chunks in the wrong language. This leaves 45{,}465 isiXhosa and 22{,}927
English chunks, split by issue 357/77/77 into train/validation/test, stratified by year.

\textbf{Modernisation.} From 3.3M modern isiXhosa tokens \TBD{verify} (Vukuzenzele \citep{vuk},
Wikipedia, OPUS-100 \citep{opus}) we build a frequency list $f$. \emph{Aspiration} restores unwritten
aspiration ($t,p,k,r,c,q,x$ $\to$ $+h$): $w$ becomes its most frequent variant $w'$ only if
$f(w)\le\tau_o$ and $f(w')\ge\tau_v$. \emph{Prefix} joins prefixes written apart (\emph{e Palamente}
$\to$ \emph{ePalamente}) only if the joined form, in any aspirated spelling, has $f\ge\tau_v$ (before a
lowercase word, also five times the bare word's). $(\tau_o,\tau_v)$ maximise $F_{0.5}$ on hand-checked
validation words, as over-correcting an author is the costlier error \TBD{tuned values}.

\textbf{Pairs.} Chunks are embedded with LaBSE and multilingual-E5 \citep{e5}. Mutual nearest neighbours
across an issue's two sections are candidate translations; we keep LaBSE candidates with similarity
$\geq$0.55 \TBD{lowest threshold with validation precision $\geq$0.9}, and repeat at $\geq$0.65. E5,
whose similarities lie between 0.83 and 0.87, keeps the same share of its candidates.

\textbf{Removing language, measuring content.} Let $P$ be the translated pairs in training issues.
Because a pair shares its content, its difference is language:
\begin{equation}
D=\big[\,x^{\mathrm{xh}}_i-x^{\mathrm{en}}_i\,\big]_{i\in P},\qquad
\tilde{x}=x-V_k^{\top}V_k\,x,
\end{equation}
where $V_k$ holds the top-$k$ right singular vectors of $D$. A class-balanced logistic-regression probe
predicts each chunk's section from $\tilde{x}$. Its balanced accuracy on held-out pairs is the
\emph{floor} (language signal that survives), and the content gap is
$\Delta=\BA_{\mathrm{all}}-\BA_{\mathrm{pairs}}$. $k\in\{0,1,2,4,\dots,64\}$ is the smallest value
whose validation floor is $\le$0.55, and $C\in\{10^{-2},\dots,10^{3}\}$ is tuned on validation. A cosine
kNN probe repeats the measurement, as a linear probe cannot see differences that mean-centring leaves.
For H2, variants reuse the raw pairs and $k$; we report paired bootstrap CIs over test issues
($B{=}1000$) and call a variant equivalent to raw if the 90\% CI of its change in $\Delta$ lies within
$\pm$0.02 (two one-sided tests, $\alpha{=}0.05$). Alignment uses pairs matched by shared names and
numbers \TBD{hand-verified}, which no embedding selected. On synthetic data with a planted gap
$\delta$, we compare against an oracle that sees content only, with clean and with 40\% false pairs.

\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{The method recovers known gaps.} On synthetic data, without language removal the probe
separates translated pairs perfectly (floor 1.00, gap 0): it detects language, not content.
Mean-centring reports a gap of 0.000 at every $\delta$, as expected for a linear probe. The pair-based
method tracks the oracle (Figure~\ref{fig:syn}; 0.097 vs 0.099 at $\delta{=}0.4$) and, with 40\% false
pairs, underestimates rather than inflates it (0.045 vs 0.059).

\textbf{What modernisation changes.} With provisional thresholds, the full normaliser rewrites
\TBD{12.4\%: update after re-run} of the 3.7M alphabetic isiXhosa tokens, 7.8\% by restoring aspiration
(\emph{apa}$\to$\emph{apha}, \emph{pakati}$\to$\emph{phakathi}). \TBD{Normaliser precision/recall on
held-out words.} Aspiration alone rewrites 149 likely proper names (capitalised in $\ge$90\% of uses),
including \emph{Xosa}$\to$\emph{Xhosa},
\emph{Tembu}$\to$\emph{Thembu}, \emph{Mtata}$\to$\emph{Mthatha} and \emph{Tixo}$\to$\emph{Thixo}.

moves: modernisation acts on vocabulary, not segmentation. isiXhosa needs about two-thirds more LaBSE subword tokens per word than English,
in 1880s and modern text alike.

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{The control is the method.} Mean-centring silently assumes that H1 is false; pair-based
removal does not, and recovers planted gaps, conservatively when pairs are noisy. Since Imvo's English section centred on the
translated editorial \citep{schoots}, a positive gap should reflect what the English half left out,
such as letters, local news and notices \TBD{confirm with the clusters}.

\textbf{Modernisation is a forking path.} \TBD{Interpret H2: safe for this question, or a conclusion
that hinges on preprocessing.} The ablation shows which rule drives this, and
Table~\ref{tab:tools} why: modernisation acts on vocabulary, not on segmentation.

\textbf{What ``fixing'' text costs.} The normaliser rewrites the names of a people (\emph{Xosa}), a
community (\emph{Tembu}), a town (\emph{Mtata}) and God (\emph{Tixo}) into today's forms. These are
correct modern spellings, but they replace how the authors wrote their own names and beliefs, which
the archive itself preserves \citep{schoots}. We recommend that analyses of historical
African-language text report a \emph{normalisation-sensitivity} check: results on raw and on
normalised text.

\textbf{Limitations.} Pairs are concentrated in some genres \TBD{genre mix} and 43\% come from 1885--86.
Labelling was done by \TBD{the team's isiXhosa reading level}. Equal-length chunks hold more content in
isiXhosa than in English, which caps pair similarity. Residual OCR noise is unmeasured \citep{schoots}. We measure content, not editorial
intent.

\section{Conclusion}
\looseness=-1
Removing language with translated pairs keeps the content difference that mean-centring erases, and
spelling modernisation should be reported and ablated, not treated as cleaning. \TBD{What H1 and H2
