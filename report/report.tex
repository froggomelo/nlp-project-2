\section{Abstract}
{
\bf
\looseness=-1
\emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} (1884--1894) printed a large isiXhosa section and a smaller English one that
was not a full translation of it. This study tests whether a multilingual encoder (LaBSE) can see this difference
once language is removed, and whether modernising the 1880s spelling changes the answer. The newspaper's
own translations are the control: a classifier's accuracy on them, the floor, counts as language, not
content. Above that floor the sections clearly differ, in every setting tried. Modernising has an
inconclusive effect on this gap and moves the floor, not accuracy on all text: spelling changes how well
the tool reads the paper, not what it says. It also rewrites the names of peoples, places and God.
}
\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{Keywords:}
historical text normalisation; multilingual sentence embeddings; isiXhosa
\end{quote}

\section{Introduction} \label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
\emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded by John Tengo Jabavu in 1884, was the first African-owned and run
newspaper in South Africa. It was printed mainly in isiXhosa, with an English translation of the editorial
and, at times, other English articles, letters and adverts \citep{schoots}. A bilingual reader can see that
the sections differ; this study asks whether NLP tools, built mostly from modern, high-resource text \citep{joshi},
can see it too, because that decides whether archives like this can be studied at scale.
The tools also invite researchers to ``clean'' the isiXhosa first by modernising its spelling: the archive writes
\emph{Tixo}, \emph{apa} and \emph{umteto} where modern isiXhosa writes \emph{Thixo}, \emph{apha} and
\emph{umthetho}.

\textbf{Research question.} \emph{If the part of a multilingual encoder's embeddings that only
encodes language is removed, can a classifier still tell Imvo's isiXhosa and English sections apart by their content,
and does modernising the 1880s isiXhosa spelling change how large that content difference looks?} \textbf{H1:} a classifier tells the sections apart more
accurately on ordinary chunks (60-word pieces of a page) than on translated pairs, which say the same thing; this gap, the measure of content difference, is expected to be above zero. \textbf{H2:} modernising lowers the accuracy on
translated pairs (the floor), because the encoder reads modern spelling better, but leaves the gap unchanged,
within $\pm$0.02 (measurement invariance); otherwise, a cleaning step changes the conclusion.

\subsubsection{Background}
\looseness=-1
The archive was OCR-corrected by first-language isiXhosa speakers and deliberately not spell-checked,
because 1880s spelling differs from today's \citep{schoots}. Historical normalisation is usually judged by
the accuracy of its spellings or by task scores \citep{bollmann}, rarely by whether it changes a
conclusion, which makes it a ``forking path'' \citep{gelman}. LaBSE \citep{labse} was trained to match
translations in 109 languages, isiXhosa among them, but still encodes language, which can be removed by subtracting each language's average embedding \citep{libovicky} or by
projecting out a few directions \citep{lir,inlp}. Subtracting averages also removes any average content
difference, so a linear classifier would find none. Following
probing work that asks for a control \citep{hewitt}, this study uses the newspaper's own translations.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\setlength{\dbltextfloatsep}{6pt plus 2pt minus 2pt}

  \caption{\looseness=-1 (a) Synthetic data (5 seeds, s.d.; dashed: 40\% false pairs). (b) Test issues, LaBSE:

\textbf{Data.} The 511 of 517 Imvo issues in the available copy with at least 1{,}000 words per language are used:
3.9M isiXhosa and 1.5M English words, close to the archive's counts \citep{schoots}. OCR artefacts
but never spelling are fixed, pages are cut into 60-word chunks, and boilerplate (text repeated across many issues,
such as standing adverts: 29.9\% of isiXhosa and 7.9\% of English chunks) and chunks in the wrong
language are dropped, leaving 45{,}465 isiXhosa and 22{,}927 English chunks. Issues are split 357/77/77 into
train/validation/test, stratified by year.

\textbf{Modernisation.} From 3.3M tokens of modern isiXhosa (88.5\% OPUS-100 \citep{opus}, the rest
Wikipedia and Vukuzenzele \citep{vuk}) a word list is built. \emph{Aspiration} adds the missing \emph{h}
after \emph{t, p, k, r, c, q, x}, but only if the old form is almost absent from the list ($\leq$2 uses) and
the new one is present ($\geq$5). \emph{Prefix} joins prefixes written apart (\emph{e Palamente}$\to$\emph{ePalamente})
only if the joined form is in the list ($\geq$5 uses). These thresholds were fixed in advance to favour precision: changing a correct word is worse than
missing a change. The rules rewrite 9.1\% of the 3.7M isiXhosa words (7.8\% by aspiration), including 191
words the authors capitalised in $\geq$90\% of non-headline uses (19{,}917 rewrites): names such as
\emph{Xosa}, \emph{Tembu}, \emph{Mtata} and \emph{Tixo}, and institutions such as \emph{Rulumente}.

\textbf{Translated pairs.} Chunks are embedded with LaBSE and, as a check, multilingual-E5 \citep{e5}. Two
chunks from the same issue, one per section, that are each other's nearest neighbour form a candidate
translation; LaBSE candidates are kept with similarity $\geq$0.55 (and repeat at $\geq$0.65). Pairs were not
hand-labelled, so this threshold is provisional, but names and numbers, which are not translated, support
it: kept pairs share three or more nine times as often as dropped ones (25.7\% vs 2.9\%). E5's
similarities are squeezed into 0.83--0.88, so for E5 the same share of candidates is kept.

\textbf{Measuring content.} A translated pair says the same thing twice, so the difference between its
embeddings is mostly language. These differences are stacked for training pairs and their top $k$
directions are removed from every embedding:
\begin{equation}
D=\big[\,x^{\mathrm{xh}}_i-x^{\mathrm{en}}_i\,\big]_{i\in P},\qquad
\tilde{x}=x-V_k^{\top}V_k\,x,
\end{equation}
where $V_k$ holds the top-$k$ right singular vectors of $D$. A class-balanced logistic regression then
predicts each chunk's section, scored by balanced accuracy (BA; chance is 0.5). Its BA on held-out
translated pairs is the \emph{floor}, and the \emph{gap} is $\Delta=\BA_{\mathrm{all}}-\BA_{\mathrm{pairs}}$. On validation, $k\in\{0,1,2,4,\dots,64\}$ is chosen as the smallest with a
floor $\leq$0.55 (else the largest), as is the regularisation strength $C\in\{10^{-2},\dots,10^{3}\}$. A cosine $k$-nearest-neighbour (kNN) probe repeats the
measurement as a non-linear check. For H2, variants reuse
the raw pairs and $k$, and a variant is equivalent to raw if the 90\% CI of its change in $\Delta$ (1{,}000
bootstraps over test issues) lies within $\pm$0.02 (two one-sided tests, $\alpha{=}0.05$). Topics are
$k$-means clusters of $\tilde{x}$, fitted without section labels. \textbf{Synthetic check.} To test the method on a known answer, embeddings are simulated as a
content (topic) vector plus a language vector. English chunks draw topics evenly; a share $\delta$ of
isiXhosa chunks draws from only a quarter of them, a content gap that is \emph{planted}. Translated pairs copy
isiXhosa content into English. An \emph{oracle} probe sees only the content, giving the true gap.

\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{The method finds planted gaps.} On synthetic data (Figure~\ref{fig:res}a), pair removal recovers
the planted gap (0.097 against the oracle's 0.099 at $\delta{=}0.4$) and, with 40\% false pairs,
underestimates it (0.045 vs 0.059) rather than inflating it; no removal and mean-centring report 0.

\textbf{The sections differ (H1).} Removing one language direction drops the validation floor from 0.97 to
0.59, but no $k$ brings it to 0.55, so $k{=}64$ is used (test floor 0.590, 95\% CI 0.561--0.621). Above this
floor the gap is 0.196 (0.167--0.224), or 0.258 with E5. It holds with stricter pairs (0.163), a kNN probe
(0.149; 0.046 with E5) and without number-heavy chunks such as price lists (0.185, 22\% of isiXhosa chunks
removed), and it is positive in all 56 settings run; only mean-centring with the linear probe removes
it. It is also a lower bound: the floor is 0.64 on the least similar half of test pairs but 0.54 on the
most similar, giving a gap of 0.244 (Figure~S1).

\textbf{Spelling moves the floor (H2).} No spelling variant is equivalent to raw or clearly different from it
(Table~\ref{tab:main}; with E5 the change is $+$0.013, $-$0.015 to 0.039): every 90\% CI crosses $\pm$0.02 but
includes 0. Accuracy on all chunks does not change ($-$0.002, 95\% CI $-$0.007 to 0.004); the change comes
from the floor ($-$0.019; Figure~\ref{fig:res}b), and translated pairs move closer together (similarity
$+$0.005, 0.003--0.007) although they were chosen on raw text. Time behaves the same way for LaBSE:
all-chunk accuracy and the floor both rise over the decade (Figure~\ref{fig:res}b), and against each
period's own floor $\Delta$ changes by $-$0.012 (CI $-$0.073 to 0.048); for E5 it is
inconclusive. H2 is neither supported nor rejected.

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
An encoder can see that Imvo's readerships got different content, and the
newspaper's own translations show it by separating how well the tool reads the paper from
what the paper says. The changes tested moved the first, not the second.
Modern spelling turns words absent from modern text into ones that occur in it (31.5\% of words
absent before aspiration, 24.5\% after), so an isiXhosa chunk lands
closer to its English translation and less language is left for the probe, while all-chunk accuracy stays
put because the content did not change. The floor also explains why no $k$ reaches 0.55: what remains is mostly loose pairs, which differ in
content too, so subtracting the floor makes the gap conservative.

The encoder sees this content without supervision. LaBSE was trained to match translations, not to sort
topics, yet $k$-means on its language-removed vectors separates each issue's halves twice as much as
shuffled sections (Jensen--Shannon divergence between their topic mixes, 0.141 vs 0.067). Each readership got content the other rarely saw: 16\%
of isiXhosa chunks fall in topics holding 1.9\% of English chunks, such as market prices (\emph{Inkuku 7d.\ ne
8d.\ inye}, ``chickens 7d.\ and 8d.\ each''), land notices and sport, and 32\% of English chunks in
English-majority topics such as liquor licensing (Figure~S2; silhouette 0.008: overlapping clusters). The English
section was a second paper, not just a translation.

Modernising has a cost the gap does not show: it rewrites the names of a people (\emph{Xosa}), a
community (\emph{Tembu}), a town (\emph{Mtata}) and God (\emph{Tixo}). ``Modern'' is also set by available
data (here 88.5\% OPUS-100), and tools that favour English (2.11 LaBSE tokens per isiXhosa word, 1.26 per
English one) tempt researchers to study only the English half. The conclusions
are limited by unchecked pairs and rewrites, a floor above chance and pairs mostly from page 2 (72\% vs
49\% overall); the method measures content, not intent.

\section{Conclusion}
\looseness=-1
An encoder can see that Imvo's readerships got different content, but only against the floor its own
translations give; archive studies should report this floor on raw and normalised text.
