\section{Abstract}
{
\bf
\looseness=-1
In its 1884--1894 issues, \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} printed a large isiXhosa section and a smaller English one,
not a full translation. This study asks if a multilingual encoder (LaBSE)
can see this difference once the language signal is removed from its embeddings, and whether modernising the 1880s spelling changes the answer. The newspaper's own translations are
the control: they say the same thing twice, so a classifier's accuracy on them, the floor, shows
how well it tells the sections apart with no difference in content. Above that floor the sections' content
differs, mostly in subject, and the classifier's errors show where they overlap; an isiXhosa speaker's check
of the pairs confirms it. Modernising
measurably helps the tool read, bringing translations closer, but its effect on this gap cannot be told apart
from zero: spelling changes how well the tool reads the paper, not what it finds. It also rewrites names of
peoples, places and God.
}

\small
\textbf{Keywords:}
historical text normalisation; multilingual sentence embeddings; isiXhosa

\section{Introduction} \label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
\emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded by John Tengo Jabavu in 1884, was the first African-owned newspaper in
South Africa, printed mainly in isiXhosa with an English translation of the editorial and some other items
\citep{schoots}. NLP tools are built mostly from modern, high-resource text \citep{joshi}, and invite
researchers to ``clean'' the isiXhosa by modernising it (\emph{Tixo}, \emph{umteto} $\to$
\emph{Thixo}, \emph{umthetho}).

\textbf{Research question.} \emph{With the language signal removed from a multilingual encoder's
embeddings, can a classifier still tell Imvo's sections apart by content, and does modernising the spelling
change how large that difference looks?}
\textbf{H1:} on a test set, a classifier predicting each chunk's section (a chunk is a 60-word piece of a
page) has a higher balanced accuracy (BA, the mean of its accuracy on each section; chance is 0.5) on all
chunks than on translated pairs, which are chunks matched to their translation in the other section. Pairs
share their content, so their BA, the floor, is what the classifier achieves without content. The gap
$\Delta$, all-chunk BA minus the floor, measures the content difference. H1 holds if the 95\% CI of $\Delta$,
resampled over test issues, lies above zero. \textbf{H2:} modernising lowers the
floor, as the encoder reads modern text better, but leaves $\Delta$ unchanged within $\pm$0.02
(measurement invariance); otherwise, cleaning changes the conclusion.

\subsubsection{Background}
\looseness=-1
The archive was OCR-corrected by first-language isiXhosa speakers and deliberately not spell-checked,
because 1880s spelling differs from today's \citep{schoots}. Neither spelling is neutral: written isiXhosa
was first shaped by missionary grammars made for learners rather than speakers \citep{maseko}, and
low-resourcedness is also a question of who builds tools and for whom \citep{nekoto}. Historical normalisation is judged by spelling accuracy or task scores
\citep{bollmann}, rarely by whether it changes a conclusion, making it a ``forking path'' \citep{gelman}. LaBSE \citep{labse} was trained to match translations in 109 languages,
isiXhosa among them, but its embeddings still identify the language. This can be removed by subtracting
each language's average embedding \citep{libovicky}, which also removes any average content difference,
or by projecting out a few directions \citep{lir,inlp}. As in control-based probing \citep{hewitt}, the
paper's own translations are the control.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\setlength{\dbltextfloatsep}{6pt plus 2pt minus 2pt}

\textbf{Data.} The 511 of 517 Imvo issues with at least 1{,}000 words per language are used: 3.9M
isiXhosa and 1.5M English words. Boilerplate (text repeated across issues) and chunks in the wrong language are dropped, leaving 45{,}465
isiXhosa and 22{,}927 English chunks. Issues are split 357/77/77 (train/validation/test), stratified
by year.

\textbf{Modernisation.} A word list is built from 3.3M tokens of modern isiXhosa (mostly OPUS-100 \citep{opus}; also
Wikipedia and Vukuzenzele \citep{vuk}). \emph{Aspiration} adds the missing \emph{h}
after \emph{t, p, k, r, c, q, x} only if the old form is almost absent from the list ($\leq$2 uses) and the
new form is present ($\geq$5). \emph{Prefix} joins prefixes written apart (\emph{e Palamente}$\to$\emph{ePalamente})
only if the joined form is in the list ($\geq$5 uses). Thresholds were fixed in advance to favour precision. The rules rewrite 9.1\% of the 3.7M
isiXhosa words, including 191 likely names (capitalised in $\geq$90\% of uses).

\textbf{Translated pairs.} Each chunk is embedded with LaBSE and, as a check,
multilingual-E5 \citep{e5}. Within each issue, an isiXhosa and an English chunk that are each other's closest
match by cosine similarity form a pair if their similarity is $\geq$0.55 (311 test pairs; 10{,}289 test chunks). A fluent isiXhosa speaker checked 51
of 100 sampled test pairs (27 first labelled with AI help): 31\% were translations, 29\% partial and 39\% different,
so the threshold is loose. Names and numbers support it, as translation keeps them: 25.7\% of
kept pairs share three or more, against 2.9\% of rejected candidates. E5 (similarities 0.83--0.88) keeps LaBSE's share.

\textbf{Measuring content.} A pair says the same thing twice, so the difference between its two embeddings
is mostly language. These differences are stacked for training pairs and their top $k$ directions
are projected out of every embedding:
\begin{equation}
D=\big[\,x^{\mathrm{xh}}_i-x^{\mathrm{en}}_i\,\big]_{i\in P},\qquad
\tilde{x}=x-V_k^{\top}V_k\,x,
\end{equation}
where $V_k$ holds the top-$k$ right singular vectors of $D$. A class-balanced logistic regression is fitted
on training issues and scored on test issues as in H1, giving $\Delta=\BA_{\mathrm{all}}-\BA_{\mathrm{pairs}}$.
On validation issues, $k\in\{0,1,2,4,\dots,64\}$ is set to the smallest value with a floor $\leq$0.55 (else
the largest), and the regularisation strength $C\in\{10^{-2},\dots,10^{3}\}$ is tuned (E5 picks the largest). A cosine
$k$-nearest-neighbour (kNN) classifier, which fits no weights, repeats the measurement. For H2, each spelling variant keeps the pairs and $k$ chosen on the
original text, so only the spelling changes; it is equivalent to raw if the 90\% CI of its change in
$\Delta$ (1{,}000 resamples of test issues) lies within $\pm$0.02.
Topics are $k$-means clusters of $\tilde{x}$, fitted without section labels (the checks below use a refit of them). A \emph{topic-only rule}
guesses the isiXhosa section whenever a chunk's topic is mostly isiXhosa in training issues; this and the
error checks were added after the main results, so they are exploratory. \textbf{Synthetic check.} To
test the method on a known answer, embeddings are simulated as a topic vector plus a language vector.
English chunks draw topics evenly; a share $\delta$ of isiXhosa chunks draws from only a quarter of them,
the \emph{planted} gap. Pairs copy isiXhosa content into English. A \emph{content-only} classifier, given the
topic vectors alone, measures the true gap. Language is added, as the method assumes, so this checks the method, not LaBSE.

\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{The method finds planted gaps.} On simulated data (Figure~\ref{fig:res}a) the planted gap is recovered
(0.097 vs the true 0.099 at $\delta{=}0.4$); with 40\% false pairs it is underestimated (0.045 vs 0.059), not inflated.

\textbf{The sections differ (H1).} Removing one direction drops the validation floor from 0.97 to 0.59; more
barely help, so the fallback rule picks $k{=}64$. The classifier then gives both halves of 201 of the 311
test pairs the same section, still splits 83 and swaps 27; the floor is above 0.5 only because 83 outnumber 27.
Against it, $\Delta$ is 0.196 (0.167--0.224), supporting H1, and it stays positive in every check in
Table~S1 (smallest with kNN on E5: 0.046). On 31 checked full or partial
translations, the floor is 0.53 and $\Delta$ is 0.254 (0.134--0.363); on full translations it is at
chance (0.50), so the main $\Delta$ is cautious (Figure~S1).

\textbf{The gap is mostly subject matter.} The topic-only rule reaches a BA of 0.661 (0.646--0.674) on test
issues, but only 0.523 on translated pairs, which share a topic. The classifier's mistakes follow it: chunks on a topic the other section usually covers are 34\% of test
chunks but 63\% of errors. Within a single topic the classifier still reaches 0.73.

\textbf{Spelling helps the tool read but does not detectably move $\Delta$ (H2).} Modernising measurably
helps: aspiration cuts words missing from modern text from 31.5\% to 24.5\%, and translated pairs move closer
(similarity $+$0.005, 95\% CI 0.003--0.007). Its effect on $\Delta$ cannot be told apart from zero ($+$0.018,
90\% CI $-$0.003 to $+$0.037; Table~\ref{tab:main}; E5 behaves the same), and all-chunk BA is unchanged
($-$0.002). This null is informative but not proof of equivalence: every CI crosses $\pm$0.02 (10\% of
$\Delta$), so a change that size cannot be ruled out. The floor's 0.019 fall is within noise ($-$0.043 to 0.005).

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{The classifier sorts by subject.} That the sections differ is expected \citep{schoots}; what is
new is measuring it apart from language. LaBSE only embeds each chunk;
the linear classifier on top learns which subjects each section printed. The English
section leaned towards colonial policy: commissions, land laws and liquor licensing; the isiXhosa section
towards community life: association meetings, sport, marriage, land sales, market prices
(Figure~S2).

\textbf{Its mistakes are evidence, not noise.} The classifier errs where one section printed what the other
usually did, so its errors map where the sections overlapped: 23 of 24 English land-sale notices go to
the isiXhosa section, and most isiXhosa liquor-licensing and land-law articles to the English one.

\textbf{Cleaning helps reading but costs the record.} Modernising brings
translations closer without changing all-chunk BA, which content sets. But the same rules rewrite how people named
themselves in 1884: a people (\emph{Xosa}), a community (\emph{Tembu}), a town (\emph{Mtata}) and God
(\emph{Tixo}). The tools also read English more easily (2.11 LaBSE tokens per isiXhosa word, 1.26 per
English), which tempts researchers to study only the English half; who builds them, and for whom, still
shapes what an archive can show \citep{nekoto}. Limits remain. Only 51 pairs were checked
by hand, 39\% were not translations, and no person checked the rewrites. The speaker who checked them
noted that \emph{isiXhosa asitolikwa}, ``isiXhosa cannot be translated'': even good translations adapt, turning
``a feather in its cap'' into the \emph{indwe}, the crane feather of honour (Y.~Siko, pers.\ comm.), so the
floor controls content only approximately. Topic checks
use LaBSE only; the method measures what was printed, not why. Archive studies should report this floor for both spellings.
