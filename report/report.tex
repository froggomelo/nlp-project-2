\section{Abstract}
{
\bf
\looseness=-1
In its 1884--1894 issues, \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} published an isiXhosa section and a smaller English one.
This study asks whether they differ in content once the language signal is removed from LaBSE embeddings,
and whether modernising isiXhosa spelling meaningfully changes the answer. A classifier's accuracy on the
newspaper's translations, the floor, estimates how well it tells the sections apart without much difference
in content. Performance above this floor suggests that the sections differ in content, mainly in subject
matter. Modernising the spelling brings translations slightly closer, but its effect on the content
difference is inconclusive.
}
\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{Keywords:}
historical text normalisation; multilingual sentence embeddings; isiXhosa
\end{quote}

\section{Introduction} \label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
\emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded by John Tengo Jabavu in 1884, published mainly in isiXhosa, with some
English translations and other items \citep{schoots}. NLP tools are mostly developed from modern text in
high-resource languages \citep{joshi}, which gives researchers a reason to modernise the spelling of
isiXhosa texts first, for example changing \emph{Tixo} and \emph{umteto} to \emph{Thixo} and \emph{umthetho}.
Historical normalisation is judged by spelling accuracy or task scores \citep{bollmann}, rarely by whether it
changes a conclusion, making it a ``forking path'' \citep{gelman}.

\textbf{Research question.} \emph{After removing the language signal from a multilingual encoder's
embeddings, can a classifier still distinguish between Imvo's two sections based on their content, and does
modernising the spelling change the size of this difference?}
\textbf{H1:} A classifier predicting which of the two sections a chunk (a 60-word piece of a page) comes from
should achieve a higher balanced accuracy (BA; chance is 0.5) on all test chunks than on translated pairs.
Pairs are chunks matched to their likely translation from the other section. Each pair should share the same
content, so BA on these pairs is the floor, an estimate of how well the classifier tells the sections apart
without a content difference. The difference between the two BAs, named $\Delta$, estimates the content
difference. H1 is supported if the 95\% confidence interval (CI) of $\Delta$, resampled over test
issues, lies above zero. \textbf{H2:} Modernising isiXhosa spelling should lower the floor, since the encoder
reads modern text better, while leaving $\Delta$ unchanged within $\pm$0.02 (measurement invariance). Beyond
this range, modernising changes the conclusion.

\subsection{Background}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Historical isiXhosa.} The archive's machine-read (OCR) text was corrected by
native isiXhosa speakers and deliberately not spell-checked, as 1880s spelling differs from today's
\citep{schoots}. Spelling is not neutral. Written isiXhosa was first shaped by missionary grammars made for
learners rather than speakers \citep{maseko}, and low-resourcedness is also a question of who builds tools
and for whom \citep{nekoto}.

\textbf{Removing language.} LaBSE \citep{labse} was trained on translations in 109 languages, including isiXhosa, but its embeddings still identify the language. This signal can be removed
by subtracting each language's average embedding (mean-centring; \citealp{libovicky}), which also removes average content differences, or by projecting out a few directions \citep{lir,inlp}. As with control tasks \citep{hewitt},
the newspaper's own translations are the control.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\setlength{\dbltextfloatsep}{4pt plus 2pt minus 2pt}

  \label{fig:res}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Data.} From the 517 Imvo issues in the archive files, 511 with at least 1{,}000 words per language are used. Chunks in the wrong language and text repeated across
issues are left out, leaving 45{,}465 isiXhosa and 22{,}927 English chunks. Issues are split 357/77/77
(train/validation/test), stratified by year.

\textbf{Spelling modernisation.} A word list is built from 3.3M tokens of modern isiXhosa (mostly OPUS-100
\citep{opus}, and also Wikipedia and Vukuzenzele \citep{vuk}). \emph{Aspiration} restores the \emph{h} that
1880s spelling left out after letters \emph{t, p, k, r, c, q, x}, but only when
the old spelling is almost absent from the modern word list ($\leq$2 uses) and the new one is common in it
($\geq$5). \emph{Prefix} joins prefixes written apart (\emph{e Palamente}$\to$\emph{ePalamente})
only when the joined form is in the word list ($\geq$5 uses). The rules rewrite 9.1\% of the 3.7M isiXhosa words, including 191
likely names (capitalised in $\geq$90\% of uses). \emph{Modern} spelling applies both rules, while \emph{raw} is the original.

\textbf{Embeddings and translated pairs.} Each chunk is embedded with LaBSE and, for comparison,
multilingual-E5 \citep{e5}. Within each issue, an isiXhosa and an English chunk that are each other's
closest match by cosine similarity create a pair if their similarity is $\geq$0.55 (622 of the 10{,}289
test chunks form 311 test pairs). Of 100 sampled test pairs, a fluent isiXhosa speaker labelled 24 herself and reviewed 27 that
a team member had labelled with AI translations and suggested labels. Of these 51, 31\% were translations,
29\% partial and 39\% different, suggesting that the 0.55 threshold is too loose. Her own labels find more
pairs different (54\% vs 26\%), so the reviewed ones may lean towards the AI, though $\Delta$ on pairs labelled translation or partial is similar (0.24, 0.26).

\textbf{Removing the language signal (pair removal).} The differences within training pairs, which are mostly language, are stacked and their top $k$
directions are projected out of every embedding:
\begin{equation}
D=\big[\,x^{\mathrm{xh}}_i-x^{\mathrm{en}}_i\,\big]_{i\in P},\qquad
\tilde{x}=x-V_k^{\top}V_k\,x,
\end{equation}
where $V_k$ holds the top-$k$ right singular vectors of $D$. A logistic regression, weighted so both
sections count equally, is trained on training issues to predict each chunk's section. Two settings are chosen on validation
issues: the number of directions removed, named $k$, is the smallest value in $\{0,1,2,4,\dots,64\}$ that brings
the floor to 0.55 or below (64 if none does, as here), and the regularisation strength $C\in\{10^{-2},\dots,10^{3}\}$
is chosen for the highest BA.

\textbf{Spelling effects.} For H2, each spelling variant keeps the pairs and $k$ chosen from the original
text, so only spelling is changed. The variant is equivalent to raw if the 90\% CI of its change in $\Delta$ lies within $\pm$0.02.

\textbf{Topic clustering.} Topics are $k$-means clusters of the language-removed embeddings, fitted without section labels. To
test how much of $\Delta$ is subject matter alone, a \emph{topic-only rule} is applied. It assigns a chunk to the isiXhosa
section if its topic's isiXhosa share in training issues is above the overall share (as in Figure~S1).
This, and the check of where errors fall, were performed after the main results, so they are
exploratory.

\textbf{Synthetic check (planted gap).} Each simulated chunk is a topic vector plus a
language vector. A content difference is planted: English chunks use all topics evenly,
while a share $\delta$ of isiXhosa chunks use only a quarter of topics. Simulated pairs copy isiXhosa content into English, and a classifier on topic vectors alone measures the true $\Delta$. This checks the language removal and the floor, not LaBSE.


\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{The method recovers planted gaps.} Tested first on simulated data with a known answer
(Figure~\ref{fig:res}a), the method recovers $\Delta$ (0.097 vs true 0.099 at $\delta{=}0.4$). With 40\% false
pairs (unrelated chunks recorded as translations), it shrinks (0.045 vs a true 0.059). But real false pairs may
behave differently, as they are paired for looking similar.

\textbf{The classifier is above the floor (H1).} On Imvo, the classifier reaches 0.786 on all test chunks but only 0.590 on translated pairs, so $\Delta$ is 0.196 (0.167--0.224), supporting H1. Of 311 test pairs, it gives both halves one label for 201 pairs (it cannot tell which is the translation), correctly tells them apart for 83 and labels both the wrong way round for 27. $\Delta$ is positive in every Table~S2 setting (smallest 0.046) and both time periods (Figure~\ref{fig:res}b), but its size depends on the pairs used (0.117 to 0.286 across the pair sets in Table~S2). No $k$ reached the 0.55 target. Removing one direction lowered the test floor from 0.977 to 0.593, and removing more barely changed it. Removing language with only pairs of similarity $\geq$0.65 raised the floor to 0.725, as only 362 training pairs remain.

\textbf{Topic accounts for much of $\Delta$.} A topic-only rule reaches 0.661 on test chunks, but only 0.523 on
translated pairs, whose halves should share a topic. Topic accounts for about 70\% of $\Delta$ (0.138 of 0.196; Table~S2).
Chunks on topics the other section usually covers are correctly predicted 59\% of the time, against 88\% for
chunks on their own section's usual topics. Topic is not everything, though, as within a topic the classifier still averages 0.73 (over 24 topics).

\textbf{Spelling's effect on $\Delta$ is unresolved (H2).} Aspiration cuts words
missing from modern text from 31.5\% to 24.5\%, and modernising moves translated pairs closer (similarity $+$0.005). Yet its effect on $\Delta$ cannot be told apart from zero ($+$0.018, 90\% CI $-$0.003 to
$+$0.037; Figure~\ref{fig:res}b, Table~S1; likewise for E5 encoder), and the floor's fall (0.019) is within noise. Every CI crosses $\pm$0.02, so H2 is neither confirmed nor rejected.

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Subject matter is the clearest difference.} The sections are known to differ \citep{schoots}, so what
is new is measuring how much once language is removed, and showing that cleaning the spelling does not
detectably change the answer. The English section leans towards colonial policy: commissions, land laws
and liquor licensing; the isiXhosa section towards community life: association meetings, sport, marriage,
land sales and market prices (Figure~S1). The classifier errs where one section printed what the other
usually did: 23 of 24 English land-sale notices go to the isiXhosa section, and most isiXhosa land-law and
liquor articles go to the English one.

\textbf{Translation is an imperfect control.} The floor assumes that a translation says the same thing, yet the isiXhosa
speaker noted the saying \emph{isiXhosa asitolikwa}, ``isiXhosa
cannot be translated'', meaning even good translations adapt, turning ``a feather in its cap'' into the
\emph{indwe}, the crane feather of honour (Y.~Siko, pers.\ comm.). If LaBSE misses meaning that may sometimes be attached to culture, the floor on translations
may control content only roughly.

\textbf{Modernisation and the historical record.} Modernisation rules rewrite how people named themselves in 1884:
a people (\emph{Xosa}), a community (\emph{Tembu}), a town (\emph{Mtata}) and God (\emph{Tixo}). The tools
also read English more easily (2.11 vs 1.26 LaBSE tokens per word), which may tempt researchers to study only the English half \citep{nekoto}.

\textbf{Limitations.} Only 51 pairs were hand-checked and no one checked the rewrites. Topic checks use
LaBSE only, and the method measures what was printed but cannot say why. Future archive studies should
report the floor under both spellings.
